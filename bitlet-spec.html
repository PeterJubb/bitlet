Four buttons. Any sound. No phone required.

Bitlet is a small, friendly object that records what you say and plays it back when a button is pressed. Clip it to a backpack, stick it on the fridge, tie it to a bear. It works on its own, forever, with nothing to log into.

This document defines what the first prototype must do, what lives on the device versus the phone, and how four people divide the work between now and January.

Why we are building this

The case for the product, in the terms we will keep coming back to when the scope starts to wander.

Almost every toy that makes a noise makes someone else's noise. It arrives with the sounds already inside it, chosen by a stranger, identical in every house on the street. Bitlet arrives empty. What comes out of it is whatever a family puts into it: a grandmother reading one line of a bedtime story, a dad's terrible dinosaur impression, the dog, a joke that only works in one kitchen.

That is the whole idea, and it only works if the thing is genuinely easy. Hold a button, talk, let go. Press the button, hear it back. A four-year-old should manage that without being taught, and without anyone unlocking a phone.

So the phone is a workshop, not a remote control. It is where you trim a clip, pull in a sound, tidy a library. Then you put it in your pocket and the toy keeps working — on a plane, in a tent, in a car with no signal, three years from now when the app is long deleted.

Everything in this specification is downstream of that one decision. When a feature would make Bitlet dependent on a phone, an account, or a server, the answer is no.

The prototype goal for January. One integrated handheld unit: four working buttons, onboard recording and playback, rechargeable power, local storage, and a simple phone app that assigns and transfers sounds over Bluetooth.

What Bitlet is

Four sentences we should be able to repeat from memory.

  • A small, friendly four-button sound toy that records, stores, and plays short audio clips.
  • A programmable object you can hang on a bag, clip to a stuffed toy, or stick to a fridge.
  • Usable immediately without a phone, once sounds have been recorded or assigned.
  • Expandable through a companion app, with room for modes beyond sound playback later.

The two flows that matter

If these both feel effortless in January, the prototype has done its job. If either one needs explaining, we have a design problem, not a firmware problem.

Recording on the device

  1. Hold a button for about two seconds.
  2. Bitlet plays a short start tone and begins recording through its microphone.
  3. Speak, or capture a sound, while keeping the button held.
  4. Release. Bitlet plays a confirmation tone and assigns the clip to that button.
  5. A normal short press plays it back.

Safeguard. Hold-to-record can be switched off in the app so a favourite clip cannot be overwritten by accident. A two-step gesture may replace the long press after usability testing.

Assigning a sound from the phone

  1. Open the app and connect to the nearby Bitlet.
  2. Record a clip, choose a file, or pick something already in the library.
  3. The app trims it, normalises the level, converts it to the device format, and shows the estimated transfer time.
  4. Drag the clip onto one of the four coloured buttons.
  5. The app transfers clip and assignment over Bluetooth, then confirms playback.

Failure is part of the flow. An interrupted transfer must leave the previous working sound intact. Nobody loses grandma to a dropped connection.

Audio

Bitlet is not a hi-fi product. The target is intelligible voice, character sounds, jokes, and short effects at a useful indoor volume — no distortion, no hiss, and no effort spent on music fidelity or deep bass.

Flash. Use 32 MB onboard flash for the custom prototype if packaging and availability permit. A 16 MB part can carry the initial four clips but leaves little room for firmware, alternates, and uncompressed recordings. Removable storage is not required.

Firmware and app

Device firmware

  • Scan four buttons and classify short press, long press, and optional multi-press gestures.
  • Record microphone input and write the completed clip safely to flash.
  • Decode and play stored audio without blocking button input.
  • Maintain an assignment manifest linking each button to a clip and its metadata.
  • Expose Bluetooth services for pairing, device information, battery level, settings, file transfer, and verification.
  • Recover cleanly from interrupted recording or transfer, low battery, and corrupt clip data.
  • Provide serial logs and a simple factory test mode for prototype diagnosis.

Phone app

  • Discover, connect to, identify, and rename one nearby Bitlet.
  • Show four colour-matched button slots and the clip assigned to each.
  • Record, import, trim, rename, preview, and delete clips.
  • Convert clips to the internal device format before transfer.
  • Show transfer progress, success, and failure — and retry without losing the working clip.
  • Control volume, record lock, and basic gesture settings.
  • Keep the full library on the phone, and only active content on the device.

Bluetooth interface

Use Bluetooth Low Energy rather than Bluetooth Classic audio. Bitlet is not a wireless speaker. The app sends complete sound files and settings into local flash, and playback is then entirely local.

Service areaMinimum data
Device informationDevice ID, firmware version, hardware version, storage available
ControlPlay test, stop, volume, record lock, reboot

File transferCreate, chunk write, progress, commit, cancel, verify

How we work

  • A 30-minute team call each week, with a short written update before the call.
  • One shared task board. Every active task has an owner and a target date.
  • Architecture and scope decisions go in a short decision log, not only in chat.
  • Blockers get raised the day they appear, with the smallest test that would resolve them.
  • Demos use working hardware or software wherever possible, rather than a status description.

The January acceptance test

Run it in one sitting, on one unit, in front of the whole team. Ten steps, no partial credit.

  1. Power on Bitlet and identify its ready state without opening the app.
  2. Press each button and hear the correct stored sound, immediately.
  3. Record a new sound on the device and play it back after a restart.
  4. Connect the app, import or record a sound, trim it, and assign it to a chosen button.
  5. Transfer the sound, disconnect the phone, and play it from Bitlet.
  6. Interrupt a transfer and confirm the previous working sound is still there.
  7. Change volume and record lock in the app and confirm both persist.
  8. Run a representative play session with no resets, unsafe temperature, severe distortion, or corrupted storage.
  9. Charge over USB-C and report charging and battery state correctly.
  10. Complete a usability test with at least five people, and write down the top problems before P2.
